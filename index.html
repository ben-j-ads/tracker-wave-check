How it works

The test

Each wave's score has its own Wilson interval. The plausible range for the change combines them with Newcombe's method, which behaves better than the textbook formula when scores sit near 0% or 100%.

Wilson interval for each wave (l, u) change d = p2 - p1 lower = d - sqrt((p2 - l2)^2 + (u1 - p1)^2) upper = d + sqrt((u2 - p2)^2 + (p1 - l1)^2) p-value: pooled two-proportion z-test

Design effect shrinks each sample to an effective size: n divided by the design effect. Heavy weighting makes your sample behave like a smaller one.

Scanning many metrics

At 95% confidence, about 1 in 20 flat metrics will look like a move by luck. Scan 20 metrics and one will light up on average. Put the number you are scanning in the box and the check raises the bar with the Sidak correction: alpha = 1 - (1 - 0.05)^(1/k).

The verdicts

moved: the range excludes zero and reaches past the smallest change you would act on.

real, but small: the range excludes zero, but the whole range is smaller than you would act on.

no meaningful move: the range covers zero and sits entirely inside what you would ignore. The sample was big enough to say so.

not proven: the range covers zero and still allows a change you would care about. Collect more before you tell anyone.

Read this before you use the verdict

This assumes a simple random sample. Quotas, panels and weighting change the real uncertainty. The design effect box is a rough patch, not a fix.

It covers sampling noise only. A new panel provider, a changed question or a different field month can move a score by more than sampling error, and no calculator will see it.

A range that covers zero is not proof of no change. It means this data cannot tell. That is why the verdict says "not proven" and not "no change".

Scores near 0% or 100% need care. The intervals handle them, but small samples near the edges are shaky.
